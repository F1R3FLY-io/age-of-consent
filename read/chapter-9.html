BROOKLYN. THE POWERHOUSE ON KENT AVENUE. FRIDAY, 12:52 AM.

The house lights had been up for an hour and fifty-one minutes, and in that light there was nothing to see.

That was what Boi would remember first, later, when she tried to remember the night in order: how ordinary the room became once it was lit. The Powerhouse had been built in 1904 to make electricity for the sugar refinery next door, and for most of a century it had done nothing else, and then for thirty years it had done nothing at all, and then someone had swept out the pigeons, hung a lighting rig from the old crane rails and started selling tickets. In the dark, with Foundling’s Keeper on the stage and three thousand people breathing, it had been a cathedral. Under the work lights it was a shed. Concrete floor, sticky. Brick painted a municipal green to head height. Tall windows along the river side, black. A skylight down the whole length of the roof, black. And everywhere people sitting on the floor in their coats, in clumps, in the particular silence of a crowd that has been told to wait and has not been told for what.

Boi and Gyrl had the merch table.

Nobody had given it to them. At twenty past eleven, when the first police had finished walking the room and the second police, the ones without uniforms, had not yet arrived, the girl who had been selling T-shirts sat down on the floor behind the table and started to cry. Boi sat down next to her. Twenty minutes later the girl had been let out with the band’s crew and Boi was still there, with the table and the stacked shirts and the monitor on its stand that had spent the evening looping the album art: a blue booth, a hand flat against the glass.

The monitor now showed nine phone feeds at once.

Boi had both laptops open in front of it, and a cable she had bought for forty dollars from a man who sold cables, and an extension lead run from behind the bar. Anything that had gone up before twenty to midnight was still out there, on the platforms, under the hashtags, pinned to the map at the place where it had been shot, and she had been pulling it down for an hour as fast as the venue’s wifi would let her. The rest was coming to her another way. She had put a F1R3FLY node on her laptop months ago, for no reason she could have defended, and at eleven-fifteen she had stood on a chair and told the people near her that if they wanted their video kept somewhere nobody could switch off, they could send it to her from where they sat, and nobody had to hand anything to anyone. Forty-one people had. She had not asked twice.

Gyrl sat on an upturned crate beside her with the headphones on and eyes closed, listening.

At the doors there were milk crates of phones, hundreds of them, each in its own clear bag with a number on it, guarded by two officers who looked as if they would rather be anywhere. Gyrl’s was in one of them. Boi’s was on the table, switched off, because at eleven-nineteen it had lit up with the word DAD and she had looked at it for one ring and turned it off and had not turned it back on.

The sticks were in her back pocket. Nobody had asked for the sticks.

It was one of the lines from Age of Consent that people put in their signatures on the forum, and she had always heard it as Victoria being Victoria: dry, a little vain, the kind of thing a woman says when she walks into a room and knows exactly what she is to it. She had mouthed it before Victoria finished saying it, the way the people around her would mouth Kiki’s lyrics all night half a breath ahead of the voice, and she caught herself, and pressed her lips together, and the rest of it arrived anyway, out loud, in a voice she had only ever heard in her head.

“That’s how you know we’re the reason.”

Boi did not turn around. She could not have said how she knew where the voice was. She knew it the way you know where a note is coming from in a dark room. Everyone in this room had come because of a clip on a famous man’s channel, because two kids had said a date and a death and two names. Everyone had a reason.
