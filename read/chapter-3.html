Gyrl noticed first.

He didn't say anything. He didn't reach for Boi's arm or slow his pace or do any of the things that would have been natural and would have told the wrong person that he'd noticed. He just — shifted. A degree and a half to the left. Enough to change his angle of vision. Enough to put Boi slightly inside his peripheral range.

She picked it up from the shift.

Boi had spent her entire life learning to read the quality of male attention from thirty feet away — the difference between incidental and deliberate, between someone who happened to be going the same direction and someone who had decided to go the same direction. The man in the grey coat had decided. She clocked him in the moment her body registered Gyrl's micro-adjustment, which meant she'd been clocking him for thirty seconds before she knew she was clocking him. Threat assessment as proprioception. You learn it young.

She didn't look at Gyrl. They were walking side by side in the particular way of people who had not yet worked out the spatial grammar of being next to each other, and she was aware of exactly how much space she was giving him, and she kept her voice level and aimed straight ahead, as if she were talking to the street.

“Grey coat. Behind us.”

His answer came back the same way. Not a murmur — just a voice pitched for two feet, not four.

“I see him. There's a second one. Far side of the green.”

She didn't look. She didn't need to. She believed him absolutely. That told her something too.

“How long?”

“Since the library.”

Since the library. They'd been under observation for the entire Beinecke visit, which meant either the Beinecke had better security than a card and a cover story, or someone had been waiting for them to go in. Neither possibility was comforting.

She thought about splitting up. The math was obvious: two people were twice as hard to follow as one, each of them carrying nothing the other couldn't reconstruct. She ran the geometry in three seconds.
