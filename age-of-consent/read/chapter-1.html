Let's try an experiment. i have long had an idea for a mystery series called the Adventures of Victoria and Balthazar. It was to be an Internet savvy, Thin Man inspired series about a celebrated and glamorous globe trotting couple who just happened to always wind up where trouble was and used their considerable acumen and presence of mind to solve the cases that they got inadvertently involved in. Balthazar is a darkly, rakishly handsome bad boy turned good, a genius at foreign policy with his finger on the pulse of global political trends. He made a fortune as a fixer for the G20, yet always with a conscience and a pronounced sense of what is right.

Meanwhile, Victoria is a drop dead gorgeous redhead with sea green eyes, who is also a practicing mathematician with publications in homotopy type theory, concurrency theory, and algebraic geometry. Among her many key contributions is the development of computational models as invariants. Her first such result was to associate pi calculus processes to knots. She's also a hacker on the side, but no one in her public life knows about her secret identity as SweetVenom in the hacker forums. No one, except her partner Balthazar.

What gives this story another layer of depth is that Victoria and Balthazar are entirely narratives. They are an embodiment of the idea that faeries are faerie stories. So the story flips back and forth between the (mis?)adventures Victoria and Balthazar get into and just barely out of and the lives of two marginal GenZ's, who go by the names Boi and Gyrl.

Through some strange faerie magic possibly going back to a deal that Oberon made with William Shakespeare there are many traces of the Victoria and Balthazar narrative all over the Internet. MONACO. 11:47 PM.

The body was still warm when Victoria found it, which she considered rude.

She was wearing Valentino—ivory silk, backless, a single emerald at the throat—and she had been, until approximately four minutes ago, enjoying a very good Krug and an even better argument about whether Perelman’s proof of geometrization was philosophically more interesting than its consequences. “Balthazar,”

she said.

He materialized from the shadow of a potted olive tree, loosening his tie with one hand, martini in the other. His jacket was the particular black that other blacks aspire to.

“Cyanide?”

“His name was Dietrich Maas. Deputy trade attaché, German mission to Monaco. Which is a position,” Balthazar said, “that does not exist.”

Victoria was quiet for exactly three seconds.

“I’m going to need different shoes,”

III

SOMEWHERE OVER THE ADRIATIC. 2:15 AM.

The jet was small and fast and registered to a shell company that was registered to a trust that was, if you pulled the thread far enough, registered to a retired Finnish diplomat who had been dead for eleven years.

Victoria had her shoes off. Her laptop open. The glow of the screen made her eyes look the color of deep water.

“The Slovenian clause references a broker,” she said. “The broker is referred to only by a sigil. Not a name. A glyph.”

Balthazar looked up from his phone. “What kind of glyph?”

She turned the screen toward him. On it: a symbol that looked like a letter from no alphabet either of them had formally studied. And yet both of them felt, with a certainty that bypassed rational processing entirely, that they had seen it before.

“It’s from the Voynich manuscript,”

she said.

Balthazar was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the sea was black and enormous. “That’s not possible.”

“I know.”

“The Voynich manuscript is a medieval document. Undeciphered. Probably undecipherable.”

“I know.”

“So what you’re saying—”

“I’m saying,” Victoria said, closing the laptop, “that someone who knew the manuscript used it as a private cipher. And I’m saying that I want to see the original.” She looked at him with that particular expression he had never been able to name—too calm to be excitement, too alive to be calm. “It’s at Yale. The Beinecke.”

“We can be in New Haven by morning.”

“We can,” she said. “And I think we should be very quiet about it.”

V

NEW HAVEN. DAWN.

The Beinecke is made of marble so thin it translucents. On winter mornings, the stone glows gold from within, lit by whatever the day has left of itself.

Inside, under controlled climate and archival silence, MS 408 rests.

The Voynich manuscript. Two hundred and forty pages of a language no one has broken in six hundred years. Illustrations of plants that don’t exist. Astronomical charts that don’t correspond to any sky. Women bathing in systems of tubes that connect to nothing.

And, if you know where to look—on folio 66r, in the upper margin, in ink so faint it reads as shadow—a symbol.

The same symbol Victoria had seen in the broker’s file.

The same symbol that no camera had ever quite captured clearly.

The same symbol that, in seventeen independent accounts across four centuries, had been described—always slightly differently, always unmistakably—as resembling two figures. One dark. One bright. Dancing, or perhaps struggling.

It was impossible to tell which.

Outside, the city was waking up. Somewhere, a train from Chicago was crossing into Connecticut. Somewhere else, a flight from Nice was beginning its descent.

The manuscript waited, as it had always waited.

Patient as a faerie story.

Certain, in whatever way old things are certain, that it would not be waiting much longer.

THE HILL. NO TIMESTAMP.

First there is the silence.

Not the absence of sound—something more deliberate than that. The world doesn’t go quiet so much as it goes complete. Colors that weren’t visible before become available. The pressure in the chest opens like a hand unclenching, and for a moment the distinction between inside and outside becomes a question rather than a fact.

Three presences.

They don’t have bodies here. They have weight.

Something is spinning faster.

The vectors are young.

These feel different.

Atropos has been listening with the quality of attention that very old things bring to questions they have heard before in different forms. Tell me what you’re seeing at the origin point.

The Beinecke connection is active again. The routing pattern we flagged in 2019 has resumed. Someone accessed the digitization archive twice in the last seventy-two hours from outside the university network. The second access was three minutes after the Maas termination in Monaco.

Yes.

By two children on trains.

Yes.

VII

THE LAKE SHORE LIMITED. SOMEWHERE IN OHIO. 7:02 AM..

Boi had not slept.

This was not unusual. What was unusual was that she had not been on her phone either, which meant something was actually happening in her head rather than the performed version of something happening that she fed to the internet.

She had a window seat. Outside, in the first gray light, Ohio was doing what Ohio does in November—presenting itself in flat grays and brown, mile after mile of used-up fields and water towers with town names she’d never think about again. She found it oddly beautiful. The lack of drama in it. The way it just was without asking anything.

Her sticks were in the outer pocket of her backpack, handles up. She’d already gotten two looks from the guy across the aisle. She was used to looks. She was wearing her good jacket—the one that was cut somewhere between a flight jacket and a frock coat, deep green, slightly too large—over a black turtleneck, and her hair was doing the thing it did when she’d slept on it wrong, apparently now a style other people tried to copy on purpose. She’d seen it on TikTok. Her own TikTok. That was strange.

@boi.unfurling. 47,000 followers, 31 videos, last post eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago she had posted a three-minute clip of herself playing in the alley behind her building in Providence, the sticks moving in arcs that her gyroscopic capture turned into cascading interlocking rhythms, the beat building into something she still didn’t have a name for. 280,000 views in the first forty-eight hours and then she had simply—not posted again.

Because the V&B thing had come back up, specifically the Beinecke routing anomaly, and once it came back up she couldn’t do anything else. That was the shape of her. She had tried, once, to explain it to a therapist, who had nodded carefully and said hyperfocus and dopamine regulation. That was probably right, and it missed everything important about what it felt like, which was less like a symptom than like being called.

She opened her notebook. Paper, not digital—she thought better on paper when she was in motion; the slight friction of it kept her honest.

She had been making a list. Not a neat list. A list that sprawled and doubled back and had arrows connecting things that didn’t obviously connect.

Beinecke routing — 2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019, 2023. Every four years. Leap year pattern? No — check: 2007 is not a leap year. Four year cycle but offset by one. Why?
 
Maas — who is Maas? German. Mathematical physics background (check this). Monaco. The Slovenian clause. What pipeline? Adriatic pipeline doesn’t show up until 2021 news cycle — how does a V&B story from 2019 reference it?
 
folio 66r. Upper margin. The glyph.

She stopped. Tapped her pen against the page.

The glyph was the thing she kept coming back to. She’d found a reference to it that gyrl hadn’t seen yet—a footnote in a 1978 monograph on Voynich marginalia, written by a Finnish academic named Päivi Hämäläinen, who had noted the marking and described it as “possibly a later addition, in a different hand, the ink composition suggesting northern European manufacture circa 1890–1910.”

1890–1910.

Someone had added that glyph to a six-hundred-year-old manuscript sometime around the turn of the twentieth century.

Someone who knew what it meant.

Who were you?

Then she put the notebook away and looked out at Ohio and felt the loneliness of being twenty-three and on a train at dawn with a backpack full of questions and a pair of drumsticks that could make sounds no one had made before, going to meet a person she had never seen outside of a profile picture to look at a book nobody could read.

It felt, she thought, exactly right.

She pulled out one stick. Just one. Tapped a quiet pattern against her knee, barely audible. The gyroscopes registered the arcs anyway, logging everything to her phone even unpaired, building up a rhythm in the buffer that she would probably never use.

Probably.

She tapped the rhythm of the train. Then something against it. Then something against that.

By the time Ohio became Pennsylvania she had the skeleton of something she didn’t have a name for yet.

XI

THE HILL. NO TIMESTAMP.

The silence came.

The world went complete.

Three presences. Their weight, in the approximate space.

Lachesis spoke first. She seldom did.

The annotation appeared at 10:14 AM Eastern Standard Time. The Maas termination in Monaco occurred at 11:47 PM Central European Time. That is a sixteen hour and twenty-seven minute gap.

“We don’t timestamp things that happen in the narrative,” Clotho said. Not a correction. An observation, made carefully, as if she were touching something to test its temperature.

We do now. That’s what I’m telling you. The membrane is behaving differently. Events in the narrative are—leaving marks. In the substrate.

The silence on The Hill widened, the way a room seems to when the music stops and the walls are suddenly farther off.

What kind of marks?

The annotation is physically present. I don’t mean it exists in the story. I mean a conservator at the Beinecke is going to find it tomorrow morning during routine examination and she is going to spend the next four years of her career trying to explain how fresh ink appeared in a manuscript that has been in controlled archival storage for decades.

The chest-space of The Hill expanded. The feeling was not comfortable.

That’s new.

Yes.

How new?

I have no prior instance. I want to be accurate. There are two possible explanations. The first is that the narrative has achieved a level of density sufficient to begin physically instantiating. The second is that the vectors are already functioning as conductors and the instantiation is coming through them.

The girl is on the train. The boy is on the train.

They’re on the same train.

They don’t know about the annotation.

No. But she found the routing anomaly. And he wrote something he doesn’t remember writing.

Another pause. Longer.

Atropos, who had been the quality of attention that precedes speech, finally moved.

In 1583, a man named John Dee held the manuscript. Before it was the Voynich manuscript, before it had that name, before anyone knew what it was. He sat with it for eleven days and wrote in his diary that he felt he was being read as much as reading.

A beat.

In 1903 a woman named Ethel Granville, a botanist, was the first person to photograph the manuscript. She wrote to her sister afterward that she had the distinct impression the book had been waiting for her specifically. She destroyed all her prints within the week. She never said why.

And now.

And now two young people on a train are forty minutes from New Haven and the narrative has started writing itself into physical objects. The question isn’t whether this is significant. The question is what it signifies.

It could be the beginning of the assumption. The merge starting earlier than any prior model predicted.

It could be. Or it could be that something else is using the increased conductivity for its own purposes. The 10X Collective is not the only party with an interest in this manuscript.

The silence held that thought carefully, the way the marble of the Beinecke holds light.

Who else?

Whoever killed Maas. Whoever left the empty chair at the seventh session. Whoever needed the pipeline to look like infrastructure.

They’re in the narrative?

They may be. They may always have been. Not every shadow in a faerie story belongs to the faerie.

Clotho said nothing at all. Under her fingers the thread had changed its weave, and all of her attention went down into her hands to find out how.

She did not speak again.

Which was, as Lachesis had noted before, the most alarming thing she could do.
